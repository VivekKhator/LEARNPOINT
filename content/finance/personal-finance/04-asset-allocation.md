:::warning
Educational material, not advice.
:::

Asset allocation is how the money is split between asset classes. For a diversified portfolio it explains the large majority of the variation in returns over time — considerably more than which specific funds or stocks were chosen.

## The classes

| Class | Expected real return | Volatility | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equity | High | High | Growth over long horizons |
| Debt / bonds | Low to moderate | Low | Stability, income, dry powder |
| Cash | Negative after inflation | None nominal | Liquidity, emergencies |
| Gold | Low | Moderate | Crisis diversifier, no cash flow |
| Real estate | Moderate | Low (because unmeasured) | Income and use, very illiquid |

The reason to hold more than one is that they do not move together. The reason not to hold too many is that each one is another thing to understand, rebalance and account for.

## Horizon decides the split

The single most useful question is not "what is your risk tolerance?" but **"when do you need this money?"**

| When needed | Reasonable equity share | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 year | 0% | Any drawdown is unrecoverable in time |
| 1–3 years | 0–20% | Not enough time to wait out a bad period |
| 3–5 years | 20–40% | Some capacity to wait, limited |
| 5–10 years | 40–70% | Most historical drawdowns recovered |
| 10+ years | 60–90% | Volatility averages out; inflation is the bigger threat |

:::danger
A deadline cannot be rescheduled to suit the market. Money for a house deposit in 2028 should not be in equities in 2026, regardless of how attractive equities look — because if the market falls 35% in 2027, you do not get to wait for the recovery.
:::

## Goal-based allocation

Rather than one portfolio with one allocation, run a separate allocation per goal:

```text
Emergency fund   — on hand          → 100% cash equivalents
Car, 2 years     — ₹8,00,000        → 10% equity, 90% debt
Deposit, 5 years — ₹30,00,000       → 35% equity, 65% debt
Retirement, 28 yrs                   → 75% equity, 25% debt
```

This resolves the contradiction in a single blended number. "60% equity" across everything is simultaneously too aggressive for the car fund and too conservative for retirement.

It also makes the plan legible. When markets fall, you can see that the retirement allocation is supposed to fall, and that the car money is untouched.

## Risk tolerance versus risk capacity

- **Capacity** is objective: horizon, job stability, dependants, existing assets, insurance.
- **Tolerance** is psychological: whether you can watch a 35% decline without selling.

Both constrain. A portfolio that is theoretically optimal but gets liquidated in a panic is worse than a conservative one that is held. The honest test is not a questionnaire but your own behaviour in the last downturn — or, failing that, a smaller allocation than you think you can handle.

:::tip
A useful calibration: a 70% equity portfolio should be expected to fall roughly 30–35% in a serious bear market. Work out that amount in rupees for your actual balance. If the figure is one you would act on, the allocation is too high.
:::

## Rebalancing

Over time the winners grow into a larger share, and the portfolio becomes riskier than intended without any decision being made.

```text
Target:  60% equity / 40% debt on ₹10,00,000
After a strong equity run: ₹9,00,000 equity / ₹5,00,000 debt
                         = 64% / 36%  → sell ₹60,000 equity, buy debt
```

Rebalancing forces the mechanically correct behaviour — trimming what has run, adding to what has lagged — at exactly the moments when the opposite feels right.

Two sensible triggers:

- **Calendar**: once a year, on a fixed date.
- **Threshold**: when any class drifts more than about 5 percentage points from target.

:::note
Rebalancing is primarily a risk-control tool, not a return enhancer. Over some periods it reduces returns, because it trims the best performer. It reliably keeps the portfolio's risk near what you chose.
:::

The cheapest way to rebalance is with new contributions: direct monthly investment into whichever class is below target, and the portfolio drifts back without any selling, costs or tax.

## Tax placement

Tax rules vary by country and change, so treat this as a principle rather than a recipe: where you hold an asset can matter as much as which asset you hold.

- Tax-advantaged retirement accounts shelter growth; the assets with the highest expected growth usually belong there.
- Frequently traded or income-generating assets create taxable events in ordinary accounts.
- Long-term capital gains are typically taxed more favourably than short-term, which is another cost of frequent trading.

:::warning
Tax should inform decisions, never drive them. Holding a bad investment to avoid a tax bill is a common and expensive mistake.
:::

## Glide paths

As a goal approaches, the allocation should shift toward safety — gradually, not on the day. A retirement portfolio might reduce equity by a few percentage points a year through the decade before retirement, rather than selling a large position at whatever price happens to prevail that month.

## What to remember

- Horizon drives allocation; "when do I need this?" is the first question.
- Run separate allocations per goal rather than one blended number.
- Capacity and tolerance both bind — the plan has to survive your own behaviour.
- Rebalance annually or on a 5-point drift, preferably with new money.
- Let tax inform the plan, not dictate it.
