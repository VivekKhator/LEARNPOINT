Most financial damage is not caused by picking the wrong fund. It is caused by a small number of repeated errors, nearly all of which are predictable.

## Behavioural

### 1. Selling in a decline, buying after a rally

The most expensive habit there is. Money flows into equity funds after strong years and out after bad ones, which means the average investor's entry points are systematically worse than the fund's own return line.

**Mechanism:** automate contributions so that buying continues through declines without a decision being made, and write the allocation down in advance.

### 2. Anchoring to your purchase price

You paid ₹500; it is ₹300. "I will sell when it gets back to ₹500." The market has no knowledge of your entry price, and the share has no obligation to it.

**Mechanism:** ask the replacement question — *if I held cash today, would I buy this at ₹300?* If no, the only thing holding you is the entry price, which is information about your history, not about the asset.

### 3. Recency bias

Whatever performed best in the last two years feels like the obvious choice, right at the point where its valuation is highest and future returns are likeliest to disappoint. This drives every sector-fund boom.

**Mechanism:** compare 10-year returns, not 1-year. Rebalancing does the opposite of recency by construction.

### 4. Confusing activity with progress

Checking the portfolio daily, switching funds, adding positions — none of it correlates with returns, and each trade costs money. Studies of brokerage data consistently find the most active accounts earn the least.

**Mechanism:** a scheduled review, quarterly or annually. Remove the app from the home screen.

### 5. Loss aversion

A loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equivalent gain feels good. This produces holding losers too long (to avoid realising the loss) and selling winners too early (to lock in the gain) — exactly backwards.

## Structural

### 6. Investing before clearing high-interest debt

Chasing an uncertain 12% while paying a certain 36% is a guaranteed negative spread. Yet it is common, because investing feels productive and debt repayment feels like standing still.

### 7. Having no emergency fund

Without a buffer, every surprise becomes either a high-interest loan or a forced sale of long-term investments — frequently in a bad month, because job losses and market declines correlate.

### 8. Being underinsured

A single uninsured medical event or the loss of an earner can erase a decade of disciplined saving. Term life (if anyone depends on your income) and health cover are cheap relative to what they protect.

### 9. Over-concentration in your employer

Company shares, ESOPs, RSUs plus the salary itself means your income, your savings and your job security all depend on one firm. If the company struggles, all three fail together.

**Mechanism:** a cap — say no more than 10% of net worth in your employer's equity — and sell vested shares on a schedule rather than by judgement.

### 10. Property as a default investment

A home you live in is a lifestyle decision with financial consequences, not a portfolio holding. As an investment, property is illiquid, undiversified, costly to transact, and carries maintenance and vacancy risk. Its apparent stability comes largely from the absence of a daily price quote.

## Product-related

### 11. Bundled insurance and investment

Endowment plans and ULITs/ULIPs combine cover and investing, and typically do both worse than the separate alternatives while hiding the cost of each.

```text
Bundled:  one premium → unclear cover, unclear returns, high charges, hard to exit
Separate: term insurance (cheap, large cover)
        + index fund (low cost, transparent, liquid)
```

### 12. Regular plans instead of direct plans

In India, the "regular" version of a mutual fund includes distributor commission and typically costs 0.5–1% a year more than the "direct" version of the *identical* fund. Over 25 years that is a substantial share of the final corpus, for nothing.

### 13. Chasing guaranteed-return schemes

Any product offering returns far above government bond yields with "no risk" is either mispricing risk, hiding it, or fraudulent. The rule of 72 is a quick test: a scheme promising to double money in three years is claiming roughly 24% a year, risk-free. Nothing legitimate does that.

:::danger
"Guaranteed" and "high return" cannot both be true at the same time. If a product appears to offer both, the risk is present and undisclosed.
:::

### 14. Tax-driven decisions

Buying a poor product in March to save tax is paying ₹100 to save ₹30. Tax relief should be a tiebreaker between good options, never the reason for choosing one.

## The ones that matter most

If you avoid only three things:

1. **High-interest debt**, carried.
2. **Selling in a panic**, because no plan was written down.
3. **High ongoing costs**, which compound against you silently for decades.

Those three cause more damage than every stock-picking decision most people will ever make.

## What to remember

- The behaviour gap is real and larger than most fee differences — automate to remove decisions.
- Your purchase price is irrelevant to whether you should hold.
- Keep insurance and investing separate; keep employer exposure capped.
- "Guaranteed high return" is a contradiction, every time.
