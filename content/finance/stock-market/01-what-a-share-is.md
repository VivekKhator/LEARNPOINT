A share is a unit of ownership in a company. Owning one share of a company with ten million shares outstanding means you own one ten-millionth of it — including one ten-millionth of its future profits, and of whatever is left if it is wound up.

## What that ownership entitles you to

1. **A claim on profits**, paid out as dividends if the board declares them, or retained and reinvested in the business.
2. **A vote** at the annual general meeting, proportional to your holding.
3. **A residual claim on assets** in liquidation — residual meaning *last*. Employees, tax authorities, suppliers and lenders are all paid before shareholders see anything, which is usually nothing.

That third point is the honest description of the risk. Equity holders are at the back of the queue. They accept that position in exchange for an unlimited share of the upside, which lenders do not get.

## Where your money goes

This surprises people:

| Transaction | Who receives the money |
|---|---|
| **IPO** or new issue | The company |
| Buying on the exchange afterwards | The previous owner of the share |

The overwhelming majority of trading is the second kind. When you buy ₹50,000 of a listed company's shares, the company's bank balance does not change by one rupee. You bought from another investor who wanted out.

:::note
This is why a falling share price does not directly take cash out of a company. It raises the company's cost of issuing new equity, affects employee share compensation and management's job security — but it does not drain the treasury.
:::

## Why prices move

A share price is the point where the most eager buyer and the most eager seller currently agree. It therefore reflects the **aggregate expectation** of everyone trading, about everything that might affect future profits.

The practical consequence is the single most useful idea in this tutorial:

> Prices move on the difference between what happens and what was already expected.

This explains the pattern that looks irrational from outside:

- A company reports 40% profit growth and the share falls — analysts expected 55%.
- A company reports a loss and the share rises — the loss is smaller than feared, or guidance improved.
- A long-rumoured acquisition is confirmed and nothing happens — it was priced in weeks ago.

"Good news" is not a reason for a price to rise. "Better news than the price assumed" is.

## Market capitalisation

```text
market cap = share price × shares outstanding
```

A ₹50 share is not "cheap" and a ₹5,000 share is not "expensive". The price per share depends entirely on how many shares the company chose to divide itself into. A 10-for-1 split turns one ₹5,000 share into ten ₹500 shares, and changes nothing about what you own.

Market cap is the number that tells you how big the company is in the market's estimation:

| Band (illustrative, India) | Typical description |
|---|---|
| Over ₹20,000 cr | Large cap — established, widely covered, lower volatility |
| ₹5,000–20,000 cr | Mid cap |
| Under ₹5,000 cr | Small cap — higher growth potential, far higher risk and lower liquidity |

## Dividends

A dividend is cash paid out of profits. Four dates matter:

- **Declaration** — the board announces it.
- **Ex-dividend** — buy on or after this date and you do *not* receive it.
- **Record** — the register is checked.
- **Payment** — the cash arrives.

On the ex-dividend date the share price typically drops by about the dividend amount. That is not a coincidence: the company is worth exactly that much less cash than it was the previous day.

:::danger
"Dividend capture" — buying just before the ex-date to collect a dividend and selling after — does not work as a strategy. You receive the dividend and lose approximately the same amount in share price, and you have added transaction costs and a tax event. Any pattern this obvious has been arbitraged away.
:::

Mature companies with limited reinvestment opportunities pay dividends. Fast-growing companies usually do not, because reinvesting at a high rate of return beats handing cash back. Neither is automatically better.

## Your return has two parts

```text
total return = capital gain (price change) + dividends received
```

Measuring only the price change understates the return of a dividend-paying company. When comparing investments, always compare *total* return, and prefer a "total return index" over a price index for the same reason.

## What to remember

- A share is a residual claim — last in the queue, unlimited upside.
- Buying on an exchange sends money to another investor, not to the company.
- Prices move on surprises relative to expectations, not on news itself.
- Share price alone tells you nothing about company size; market cap does.
- Dividends come out of the share price, not out of thin air.
