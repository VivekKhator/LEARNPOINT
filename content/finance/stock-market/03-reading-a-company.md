:::warning
Educational material, not advice. Ratios described here are starting points for questions, never buy or sell signals.
:::

A company's financial position lives in three statements:

- **Income statement** — revenue and profit over a period. Did it make money?
- **Balance sheet** — assets, liabilities, equity at a moment. What does it own and owe?
- **Cash flow statement** — cash actually moving. Could it pay its bills?

The third is the one amateurs skip and professionals read first.

## Size and growth

```text
Revenue              — the top line, before any costs
Net profit           — what is left after everything, including tax
Revenue growth %     — year on year, and the 3–5 year trend
```

Look at the trend, not a single year. Three years of 20% revenue growth with flat profit means costs are rising just as fast — the growth is being bought.

## Valuation

### Earnings per share and P/E

```text
EPS = net profit ÷ shares outstanding
P/E = share price ÷ EPS
```

P/E answers: how many rupees am I paying per rupee of annual profit? A P/E of 25 means 25 years of current earnings to repay the price, if nothing grows.

:::danger
A low P/E is not a bargain signal. Markets price in expectations, so a P/E of 8 against a sector at 25 normally means the market expects earnings to *fall*. Sometimes the market is wrong; often it is not. Treat a low P/E as a prompt to find out why, never as a conclusion.
:::

P/E is also meaningless when:
- Earnings are negative — there is no ratio.
- Earnings are at a cyclical peak — a commodity producer looks cheapest at the top of its cycle.
- A one-off item (an asset sale, a writedown) distorts the denominator.

### Price to book

```text
P/B = market cap ÷ shareholders' equity
```

Useful for banks and asset-heavy businesses, where book value approximates what is really there. Near-useless for a software company whose value is people and code, neither of which appears on a balance sheet.

## Profitability

```text
Operating margin = operating profit ÷ revenue
Net margin       = net profit ÷ revenue
ROE              = net profit ÷ shareholders' equity
ROCE             = EBIT ÷ (equity + debt)
```

Margins show pricing power. A business holding 25% operating margins for a decade has something competitors cannot easily copy.

ROE measures return on owners' money. The trap is that it responds to leverage: buy back equity with borrowed money and ROE rises while the business becomes more fragile. **ROCE** includes debt in the denominator and is the harder number to flatter — which is why experienced analysts reach for it.

## Debt

```text
Debt-to-equity   = total debt ÷ shareholders' equity
Interest cover   = EBIT ÷ interest expense
```

Interest cover is the one to look at first. Below about 3, a bad year starts threatening the ability to service debt. Below 1.5, the company is working for its lenders.

Debt is not inherently bad — a utility with predictable cash flows can carry far more than a cyclical manufacturer. Compare against the sector, not against zero.

## Cash flow — the honest statement

```text
Operating cash flow  — cash generated by the business itself
Capital expenditure  — cash spent on plant, equipment, property
Free cash flow       = operating cash flow − capex
```

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Revenue can be booked when an invoice is raised, long before payment arrives.

:::danger
**If net profit rises every year while operating cash flow stagnates or falls, stop and investigate.** The usual explanation is receivables growing faster than sales — the company is booking revenue it has not collected. This divergence has preceded a large share of accounting scandals, and it is visible to anyone who looks.
:::

Healthy pattern: operating cash flow roughly tracks or exceeds net profit over several years.

## A ten-minute checklist

1. **Revenue and profit, five years.** Growing, flat, or declining?
2. **Operating cash flow versus net profit.** Do they move together?
3. **Interest cover.** Comfortably above 3?
4. **ROCE.** Consistently above the cost of capital, or drifting down?
5. **Promoter/insider holding, and any pledging.** Falling insider stakes and pledged shares are both warnings.
6. **Dilution.** Has the share count been rising? Your slice shrinks every time it does.
7. **P/E and P/B against the sector.** Not to judge, but to generate the question "why is this different?"
8. **Auditor and governance.** Frequent auditor changes, qualified opinions and related-party transactions deserve attention.

## What this does not tell you

Everything above is backward-looking. The share price reflects expectations about the *future*: competition, regulation, technology, management quality. None of it shows up in a ratio.

:::tip
This is the honest case for index funds. Reading statements well is a full-time skill competing against people who do it full-time with better data. Understanding fundamentals is still worth your time — it tells you what you own and makes financial news legible — but understanding them is not the same as having an edge.
:::

## What to remember

- Cash flow over profit; the gap between them is the most useful warning sign available.
- A low P/E is a question, not a bargain.
- ROCE resists the leverage flattery that inflates ROE.
- Interest cover below 3 is where debt starts to matter.
