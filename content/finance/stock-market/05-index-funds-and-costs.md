## What an index fund is

An index is a defined list of securities with defined weights — the Nifty 50, the S&P 500, a total-market index. An index fund holds those securities in those weights and does nothing else. There is no judgement about what to buy, which is exactly the point: no judgement means no research department and no manager's salary, so the fee can be a fraction of an active fund's.

Mechanically:

- **Index fund**: you transact with the fund house at the day's closing NAV.
- **ETF**: the fund trades on the exchange. You pay the bid-ask spread and may buy at a small premium or discount to NAV.

## Cost compounds against you

This is the single most reliable idea in investing, because it is arithmetic rather than forecast.

₹10,00,000 invested for 25 years at a 10% gross return:

| Expense ratio | Net return | Final value | Lost to fees |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10% | 9.90% | ₹1,05,70,000 | ₹2,70,000 |
| 0.50% | 9.50% | ₹96,30,000 | ₹12,10,000 |
| 1.50% | 8.50% | ₹76,90,000 | ₹31,50,000 |
| 2.25% | 7.75% | ₹64,70,000 | ₹43,70,000 |

A 2.15 percentage-point fee difference consumes about 40% of the final corpus. The fee is charged on the whole balance every year, including on the returns the earlier fees would have earned.

:::tip
Fees are the only variable in investing you control with certainty. You cannot choose next decade's returns. You can choose to pay 0.2% instead of 1.8%, and that choice is worth more than most stock picking.
:::

## The arithmetic of active management

William Sharpe's argument is short and hard to escape:

1. The market is the sum of all investors' holdings.
2. Passive investors hold the market in market weights, so they earn the market return minus a tiny fee.
3. Therefore active investors, *in aggregate*, also hold the market, and before costs earn the market return.
4. Active costs are higher — research, salaries, higher turnover and trading costs.
5. Therefore the average actively managed rupee must earn **less** than the average passive rupee, after costs.

This says nothing about whether skilled managers exist. It says the average must trail, by roughly the cost difference.

The evidence agrees. SPIVA's long-running scorecards consistently find that over 10–15 year horizons, 80–90% of active funds underperform their benchmark in most major markets. Persistence studies find that top-quartile performance in one period predicts little about the next.

:::note
Active management has better odds in less efficient corners — small caps, some emerging markets, distressed debt — where information is scarcer. Even there, the fee hurdle is real, and identifying the right manager *in advance* is its own hard problem.
:::

## Reading the real cost

The expense ratio is the headline figure, not the total:

| Cost | Where it shows up |
|---|---|
| Expense ratio | Deducted daily from NAV |
| Exit load | On redeeming within a set period |
| Transaction costs inside the fund | Invisible; higher with high turnover |
| Tracking error | Shortfall against the index |
| Bid-ask spread (ETFs) | Each purchase and sale |
| Taxes | On gains, and on dividends in some regimes |

A fund with a 0.2% expense ratio and 1% tracking error is more expensive than one at 0.5% with 0.1% error. Compare *delivered* returns against the index, not the fee alone.

Direct plans are worth a specific mention: in India, the "direct" version of a mutual fund excludes distributor commission and typically costs 0.5–1% less per year than the "regular" version of the identical fund. Same portfolio, same manager, lower fee.

## A reasonable default

For most people, most of the time:

1. A broad, low-cost index fund as the core of the equity allocation.
2. Regular, automated contributions — the automation matters more than the timing.
3. An allocation between equity and debt that matches when you need the money.
4. Rebalance roughly once a year back to target.
5. Leave it alone.

This is not exciting, which is its main weakness as advice and its main strength as a strategy. It requires no forecasts and no edge.

## Where index funds fall short

- **Concentration.** A cap-weighted index can become dominated by a few names at the top of a bubble. You own the bubble in proportion.
- **No downside protection.** When the index falls 40%, you fall 40%.
- **Buying at any valuation.** The fund is indifferent to price by design.
- **Index definitions matter.** "Index fund" covers everything from a total-market fund to a narrow thematic index with active-like concentration and fees.

## What to remember

- Cost is the one input you control, and it compounds against you every year.
- The average active rupee must trail the average passive rupee after costs — arithmetic, not opinion.
- Compare delivered return versus index, not the headline expense ratio.
- Direct plans cost less than regular plans for the identical portfolio.
